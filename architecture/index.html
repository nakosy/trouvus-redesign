Architecture

What runs, and where the rules came from.

Two things a buyer is entitled to check. First, what the system actually does at each stage — including what it does when a stage fails. Second, which of our rules are ours and which are a public framework's, so you can hold us to a standard we do not control.

The decision chain

Seven stages, two gates, one person.

The homepage lets you run this chain and break it. Here it stands still, annotated. Read the failure row of each stage first — a system's behaviour under failure is the part a vendor is least likely to publish and the part you most need.

STAGE 01

Data retrieval

Does
Pulls the data the run depends on from its authenticated source and binds it to the run.
Emits
A timestamped snapshot, tied to this run and no other.
On failure
No snapshot, no run. There is no cached-value fallback.
STAGE 02 · GATE

Integrity check

Does
Tests whether the retrieved data can be verified at all: source authenticated, freshness within tolerance, values internally consistent.
Emits
A pass, or a blocked state. Nothing in between.
On failure
The chain halts here. The label is BLOCKED, and no score, target, tier or buy zone may be displayed as authoritative beside it. Mock or fallback data is marked unverified and cannot produce an actionable label at all.
STAGE 03

Deterministic engine

Does
Applies explicit rules to the verified snapshot. No model judgement participates.
Emits
A regime classification. The same snapshot always produces the same classification.
On failure
The run aborts. No partial output is published — a half-finished analysis is not a cheaper analysis.
STAGE 04

Trigger math

Does
Computes trigger conditions from the regime and the snapshot.
Emits
A trigger state, reproducible from the run record alone.
On failure
Falls through to the risk check as no-trigger, never as an assumed trigger.
STAGE 05 · GATE

Risk check

Does
Tests the candidate action against risk doctrine set before the run, not during it.
Emits
A pass, a downgrade, or a block.
On failure
The action is downgraded or blocked — never silently widened. Doctrine is not renegotiated at runtime.
STAGE 06

Strict label

Does
Maps engine output onto the closed action-label set.
Emits
Exactly one of nine labels. Eight are answers; the ninth is a refusal.
On failure
If no label fits, the answer is BLOCKED. The set is not extended at runtime — adding a label requires a schema change, a contract test and a rendering test.
STAGE 07

Human decision

Does
A named person decides. The system does not act on the label.
Emits
The record of who decided and when, held where the client controls it.
On failure
There is no automated fallback, and that is the design rather than a gap. An undecided run stays undecided.
Language models sit alongside this chain rather than inside it. They explain it, summarise it, audit it, and argue with it. They are not permitted to produce the final label.
The four constraints

Applied without exception.

Two of these are principles of the Octopus Protocol, a governance framework for multi-agent AI published by the CCAIE Foundation.1 Two are ours. The diagram below shows which is which, because the difference decides who you can hold us to.

01

The verdict is deterministic

The final output of a consequential system is produced by explicit rules, not by a model's judgement. Models reason, summarise, and challenge. The label comes from the engine, and the same inputs always produce the same label.

02

Unverified input halts the chain

No verified data, no authoritative output. The system returns a blocked state rather than a plausible one — and a blocked state is not permitted to display a score, a target, or a recommendation alongside it.

03

Every claim carries provenance

Assertions link to authenticated sources, and the link survives into the record. What was asked, retrieved, generated, approved, and when — held where the client controls it, replayable after the fact.

04

No orphan autonomy

Every agent has a named human accountability owner — a person, decided before the output is produced rather than after the harm. The interface projects verified system state; it never invents a decision.

Provenance of the rules

Six principles, and what each one becomes.

The framework states properties a system must have; it does not prescribe an architecture. Each principle traces to one thing in a Trouvus system. Two are adopted verbatim as named constraints — those you can check against the published document. The two we add are marked as ours, because claiming otherwise would borrow authority the framework does not grant.

Accent marks the thing at stake — a drifted boundary, a Trouvus addition. It never decorates.
Limits

What the framework does not claim.

It does not eliminate error. Provenance and confidence scoring reduce the rate at which unfounded claims survive into use. They do not reduce it to zero.

It does not transfer professional responsibility. Mandatory human sign-off keeps accountability with the practitioner by design. That is the point of it, not a limitation of it.

It does not require any particular technology or vendor. It states properties a system must have. How they are achieved is an engineering question, and more than one architecture can satisfy them — including one that is not ours.1

Where engagements start

None of this works on an unmeasured process.

Principle 6 is the one most often skipped and the one that most reliably invalidates everything built on top of it. It is also why every engagement we take begins the same way.
