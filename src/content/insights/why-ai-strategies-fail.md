<div class="exec-summary"><div class="es-title">💡 Executive summary</div><ul><li>Most AI strategies fail in design, not execution — they succumb to the use-case shopping list, ownership without authority, and tool-led planning.</li><li>Executable strategies prioritize a small governed portfolio and assign owners with real operating authority.</li><li>Capability — data, governance, workforce skill — is budgeted as a core investment, not assumed.</li></ul></div>
<p class="lead">When an AI initiative disappoints, the post-mortem usually blames execution: the model underperformed, the data was messier than expected, users resisted the tool. These explanations are comfortable because they locate the failure downstream, in engineering or adoption. But in our research and advisory work, the pattern is consistent and uncomfortable: most failed AI strategies were unexecutable on the day they were approved. The failure was designed in.</p>
<h2 id="three-failure-patterns-that-precede-implementation">Three failure patterns that precede implementation</h2>
<h3>1. The use-case shopping list</h3>
<p>The most common artifact called an "AI strategy" is a long list of use cases collected from departmental workshops. Each item is individually plausible—chatbots for service, forecasting for supply chain, document extraction for legal—but the list as a whole has no organizing logic. It does not say what the organization is trying to become, which capabilities compound, or what will be deliberately not done.</p>
<p>A shopping list distributes effort thinly across dozens of shallow initiatives. Nothing receives the sustained data work, process redesign and governance attention that a consequential deployment demands. Eighteen months later the organization has a portfolio of proofs-of-concept and no operating change. The problem was never the individual ideas; it was the absence of a portfolio logic connecting them to strategy.</p>
<h3>2. Ownership without authority</h3>
<p>AI initiatives change how work is done, which means they redistribute decisions, budgets and sometimes headcount. Yet responsibility for AI is routinely assigned to a function—innovation, IT, a newly hired head of AI—that has enthusiasm and expertise but no authority over the operating units whose work must actually change.</p>
<p>The result is a predictable stalemate. The AI team can build demonstrations but cannot mandate process change. Business units can ignore the demonstrations because nothing in their incentives requires otherwise. Strategy documents that do not resolve this authority question—explicitly, with named executive owners accountable for operating outcomes rather than technical delivery—have already failed. They just have not reported it yet.</p>
<h3>3. Tool-led planning</h3>
<p>The third pattern begins with a technology and searches for a justification: "We have enterprise licenses—what should we do with them?" Tool-led planning feels like progress because procurement is fast and visible. But it inverts the strategic sequence. The question that creates value is not "where can we use this tool?" but "which decisions and workflows, if improved, would change our competitive or operating position—and is AI the best instrument for improving them?"</p>
<p>When the tool comes first, the organization optimizes for usage metrics: seats activated, prompts issued, hours of training delivered. Activity substitutes for outcome. Meanwhile the genuinely valuable opportunities—often unglamorous workflow and decision redesign—remain unexamined because no vendor is selling them.</p>

<p>These are not failures of intelligence. They are failures of institutional design under uncertainty. Executive teams face acute pressure to "have an AI strategy" on a board timeline measured in weeks. Producing a use-case list is fast; building portfolio logic is slow. Assigning AI to a new function avoids hard conversations about operating authority; resolving ownership forces them. Buying tools produces immediate, announceable action; diagnosing decision architecture does not.</p>
<p>In other words, every failure pattern is the path of least organizational resistance. That is precisely why they recur, and why avoiding them requires deliberate discipline rather than better intentions.</p>
<h2 id="what-a-strategy-that-survives-contact-with-implementation-contains">What a strategy that survives contact with implementation contains</h2>
<p>Across research and practice, executable AI strategies share five properties. First, they are anchored in a small number of strategic theses—clear statements of where AI changes the economics or quality of the business, such as "our margin problem is quoting accuracy, and decision support in pricing is worth more than any customer-facing chatbot." Second, they prioritize ruthlessly: a ranked portfolio scored on value, feasibility, risk and organizational readiness, with an explicit "not now" list that is as important as the approved list.</p>
<p>Third, they name accountable owners with operating authority, and define success in operating terms—cycle time, decision quality, error rates, revenue effects—rather than delivery milestones. Fourth, they treat capability as a first-class investment: data foundations, governance, and workforce skill are budgeted alongside technology, not assumed. Fifth, they are staged. Every initiative passes through a bounded pilot with explicit hypotheses and a pre-committed scale-or-stop decision, so that learning accumulates institutionally instead of evaporating with each project team.</p>
<div class="pullquote">A real strategy constrains choices. If nothing would change without it, it is a description of enthusiasm.</div><h2 id="a-diagnostic-question-for-your-current-strategy">A diagnostic question for your current strategy</h2>
<p>If you want a rapid test of whether your AI strategy will fail before implementation begins, ask one question of it: <em>"If this document disappeared tomorrow, would any operating decision in the business change?"</em> A real strategy constrains choices—it tells budget holders what to fund, managers what to redesign, and teams what to stop. If the honest answer is that nothing would change, what you have is not a strategy but a description of enthusiasm.</p>
<p>The encouraging news is that the failure patterns are visible early, which means they are correctable early. A disciplined diagnosis of strategic priorities, decision architecture, ownership and readiness—before tools, before pilots—costs a fraction of a single failed deployment. Most organizations do not need more AI activity. They need the strategic system that makes activity cumulative.</p>
