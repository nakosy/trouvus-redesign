<p class="lead">Nearly every organization we encounter has a use-case inventory: forty, eighty, sometimes two hundred candidate applications of AI gathered through workshops and vendor conversations. Very few have a portfolio. The distinction is not semantic. An inventory is a record of imagination; a portfolio is an instrument of governance. Converting one into the other is among the highest-leverage exercises available to an executive team—and it can usually be done in weeks, not quarters.</p>
<h2 id="why-the-conversion-matters">Why the conversion matters</h2>
<p>Use cases evaluated one at a time invite three systematic errors. Local optimization: each department champions its own candidates, so investment mirrors internal politics rather than enterprise value. Capability blindness: initiatives are approved without asking whether the data, skills and governance they presuppose exist, so feasibility surprises arrive after budgets are committed. And strategic drift: because nothing connects the initiatives, even successful ones fail to accumulate into a distinctive capability that competitors cannot easily copy.</p>
<p>A portfolio corrects all three by forcing comparison. Every candidate is scored on the same dimensions, sequenced against the same constraints, and reviewed by the same governing body. Value stops being asserted and starts being argued.</p>
<h2 id="the-five-scoring-dimensions">The five scoring dimensions</h2>
<h3>Value</h3>
<p>What operating or strategic outcome improves, by how much, and how would we know? Value should be expressed in the organization's native metrics—contribution margin, cycle time, error cost, customer retention—not in generic "efficiency" language. Initiatives that cannot articulate a measurable outcome are not ready to be scored; they are ready to be clarified.</p>
<h3>Feasibility</h3>
<p>Feasibility is mostly a data and process question, not a model question. Is the required data captured, accessible and lawful to use? Is the workflow the AI must sit inside stable enough to redesign? Modern models are rarely the binding constraint; the organization's information architecture usually is.</p>
<h3>Strategic relevance</h3>
<p>Two initiatives with identical ROI are not equal if one builds a capability central to how the organization intends to compete and the other automates a peripheral task. Strategic relevance asks: does this initiative strengthen a capability we want to be known for? Does it create data or learning that makes subsequent initiatives cheaper? Portfolio value lives in these interdependencies.</p>
<h3>Risk</h3>
<p>Risk spans model behaviour (error rates and their consequences), compliance and privacy exposure, vendor and concentration risk, and organizational risk—the damage a visible failure would do to adoption appetite. Risk scoring is not about avoiding risk but pricing it, so that high-risk initiatives carry proportionate controls and are not sequenced first.</p>
<h3>Readiness</h3>
<p>Readiness asks whether the receiving organization can absorb the change: an accountable owner with operating authority, workforce capability, management bandwidth and governance capacity. A high-value, feasible initiative landing in an unready unit fails as reliably as a technically weak one. Readiness is also the dimension leadership can most directly improve, which makes it the natural focus of pre-pilot investment.</p>
<h2 id="from-scores-to-sequence">From scores to sequence</h2>
<p>Scoring produces a ranking; a portfolio requires a sequence. We recommend structuring the first horizon around three roles. Proof engagements—one or two initiatives chosen to demonstrate measurable operating value quickly, building institutional confidence. Capability builders—initiatives whose primary return is infrastructure: data foundations, governance patterns, workforce skill that lower the cost of everything that follows. And option creators—small, cheap probes into higher-uncertainty opportunities that preserve strategic flexibility without committing scale resources.</p>
<p>Just as important is the explicit "not now" list. Declining an initiative visibly, with reasons, is what makes prioritization credible. If nothing is ever refused, scoring is theatre and the portfolio will quietly revert to a shopping list.</p>
<div class="pullquote">If nothing is ever refused, scoring is theatre — and the portfolio quietly reverts to a shopping list.</div><h2 id="governing-the-portfolio-over-time">Governing the portfolio over time</h2>
<p>A portfolio is a living instrument. Each pilot concludes with a pre-committed scale, iterate or stop decision, and its lessons re-price the remaining candidates: data quality discovered in one initiative changes the feasibility scores of five others. We formalize this in the Pilot Passport—a one-page charter defining each pilot's hypothesis, scope, owner, controls, metrics and decision rule—so that the portfolio review has evidence to govern with rather than status reports to admire.</p>
<p>Quarterly, the governing body should ask four questions. What did we learn that changes any score? Which capability gaps are now the binding constraint? Is the balance across proof, capability and option initiatives still right? And what will we now stop? An AI portfolio managed this way behaves like any well-run investment portfolio: individual positions may disappoint, but the system compounds.</p>
<h2 id="starting-from-where-you-are">Starting from where you are</h2>
<p>If you already hold a use-case inventory, the conversion path is direct: agree the five dimensions and their weights with the executive team, score the top thirty candidates in structured working sessions, sequence the first horizon around proof, capability and option roles, and stand up a governance cadence with authority to stop things. The exercise typically fits within four to six weeks. What it produces is not a document but a decision system—and decision systems, unlike documents, survive contact with implementation.</p>
