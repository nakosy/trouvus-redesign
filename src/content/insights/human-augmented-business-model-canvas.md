<div class="exec-summary"><div class="es-title">💡 Executive summary</div><ul><li>Standard business-model tools don't tell you who should do the work; the HABMC adds a three-zone triage to every activity.</li><li>Automate the verifiable, augment the judgment-heavy, and explicitly defend the human-led zone that customers are actually paying for.</li><li>The boundaries are business-model decisions — draw them deliberately, not by procurement accretion.</li></ul></div>
<p class="lead">The classic business model canvas transformed strategy conversations by making value creation visible on a single page: activities, resources, propositions, channels, relationships, revenues, costs. But it was designed for a world in which the question "who does this work—human or machine?" barely arose. In an AI-saturated economy that question sits inside every block of the canvas, and answering it badly is now one of the fastest ways to damage a business model while appearing to modernize it.</p>
<h2 id="the-core-move-a-third-dimension-on-every-activity">The core move: a third dimension on every activity</h2>
<p>The Human-Augmented Business Model Canvas (HABMC) keeps the familiar business-model structure and adds a disciplined triage. Every significant activity, decision and customer interaction in the model is classified into one of three zones.</p>
<h3>Automate</h3>
<p>Work that is high-volume, low-ambiguity, rule-expressible and verifiable belongs to machines. Scheduling, matching, routing, classification, extraction, monitoring, reconciliation. The design questions here are engineering questions: accuracy thresholds, exception handling, audit trails. The strategic error in this zone is under-automation—paying professionals to do what software does better—but it is the least dangerous error, because it is visible and correctable.</p>
<h3>Augment</h3>
<p>Work where human judgment is essential but machine leverage is large sits in the augment zone: diagnosis supported by pattern detection, proposals accelerated by drafting, research widened by retrieval, designs explored through generation. Here the design questions are socio-technical: who holds final authority, how is machine output verified, what keeps the human's underlying skill alive. This is where most professional-services and knowledge-work value now lives, and where thoughtless design quietly converts augmentation into unsupervised automation—the reviewer who no longer reviews.</p>
<h3>Human-led</h3>
<p>Some work must remain human not because machines cannot produce output, but because the value of the work is inseparable from human accountability, relationship or judgment: negotiations, safety-critical calls, ethical trade-offs, strategy, moments where a client is buying trust as much as analysis. Designating this zone explicitly matters. Without a named human-led zone, cost pressure erodes every boundary one quarter at a time, and the organization discovers too late that it has automated the parts of its proposition customers were actually paying for.</p>
<div class="grid-3" style="margin:1.6em 0"><div class="card"><div class="badge blue">Automate</div><p class="author-note">High volume · Low ambiguity · Verifiable<br><strong>Goal: efficiency &amp; accuracy</strong></p></div><div class="card"><div class="badge violet">Augment</div><p class="author-note">High ambiguity · Machine-leveraged · Human authority<br><strong>Goal: speed &amp; decision support</strong></p></div><div class="card"><div class="badge amber">Human-led</div><p class="author-note">High consequence · Relationship-driven · Strategic<br><strong>Goal: trust &amp; accountability</strong></p></div></div><h2 id="why-the-boundaries-are-strategic-not-technical">Why the boundaries are strategic, not technical</h2>
<p>The central claim of the HABMC is that these boundaries are business-model decisions. Two firms with identical technology can draw them differently and become different companies. A law firm that automates document review and augments drafting while keeping counsel human-led has one economic model; one that pushes advice itself toward automation has another—with different cost structures, different pricing logic, different risks and different talent needs. Neither is wrong in the abstract. What is wrong is arriving at either position by accretion, one procurement decision at a time, without ever choosing.</p>
<p>The canvas forces the choice to be made deliberately and in one place, where its interactions become visible: what automation in the cost structure does to the customer relationship block; what augmentation of the core activity does to the key-resources block, where the firm's expertise pipeline lives; how the human-led zone connects to the value proposition and justifies the premium.</p>
<h2 id="running-the-exercise">Running the exercise</h2>
<p>In practice we facilitate the HABMC in three passes. Pass one maps the current model honestly, including where informal automation has already crept in. Pass two triages every activity into the three zones under two constraints: each placement must cite evidence about the work (volume, ambiguity, verifiability, consequence of error), and each must state what the placement does to capability—what skills it builds or retires. Pass three stress-tests the resulting model: Where would a model failure surface, and who would catch it? Which augment-zone roles need redesigned verification? What does the human-led zone cost, and what revenue or trust does it protect? What sequence of pilots would move us from the current model to the designed one?</p>
<p>The output is not a poster but a set of commitments: an explicit boundary map, owners for each redesigned role, capability plans for the augment zone, and a pilot portfolio sequenced through the Strategy-to-Scale Framework.</p>
<div class="pullquote">Two firms with identical technology can draw these boundaries differently — and become different companies.</div><h2 id="what-the-canvas-prevents">What the canvas prevents</h2>
<p>Organizations that skip this discipline tend to converge on the same failure: they automate what is easy rather than what is wise, augment without redesigning verification, and leave the human-led zone undefended. The result is a business model that is cheaper per transaction and weaker per decade—faster throughput, thinner expertise, and a value proposition drifting toward whatever competitors can also buy from the same vendors. The HABMC exists to keep the question "what should remain human?" where it belongs: not as an afterthought of implementation, but as a first-order design choice in the business model itself.</p>
