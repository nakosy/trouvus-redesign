Evidence

One engagement, published with its limits.

We have one engagement at scale to show you. This page reports what it produced, what it failed to establish, and the finding we wrote into the client's own report that most firms would have buried.

The engagement

Thirty leaders. Ten days. Five investable pilots.

In August 2026 our principal designed and led a ten-day applied AI programme, delivered through the CCAIE Foundation, for thirty executives across an industrial holding and three of its subsidiaries — general directors, finance and economics leads, production managers, and IT.1

The brief was not to teach a technology. It was to test a hypothesis: can process owners who are not programmers take an idea to the point where a board can decide whether to fund it?

The design made evasion impossible. Each of five cross-functional teams had to name a specific step in a specific process, cost it from their own data, define the condition under which the project would be stopped, and name the person answerable for it.

The caveat that belongs beside every number above. No team measured the actual duration of its process step within the programme; durations were estimated from process description. The arithmetic is sound, the input quantity is unverified, and until it is measured these figures are illustrations rather than results. We told the client's owners so in writing, and we are telling you.
And on comparison. A larger competitor in the same sector reports an average payback of 2.4 years across 95 completed digital projects.1 Those figures are as published by the companies concerned and are not independently audited. We have deliberately not set our numbers against that one as a like-for-like result: ours are modelled from estimates, theirs are reported outcomes, and those are different kinds of claim.

The part that is harder to value and impossible to buy. Five of the holding's processes are now described as they actually run, rather than as the regulations say they run. That description did not exist before August, and no supplier sells it.

The finding

Not one team had measured its own process.

Here is what we published to the client's owners, and what most consultancies would have buried.

Every business case in the portfolio was arithmetically sound. None rested on a measured input. One team reported eight hours where the figure turned out to describe how long a document takes to travel, not how long a person works on it.

This is not a criticism of the participants. The holding has no practice of operational measurement — process durations are known by impression, not by protocol. And the implication runs well past AI: where a baseline is not measured before work begins, the effect of any project becomes impossible to confirm or refute, and every argument about results collapses into an argument about opinions.

The larger conclusion

Training does not close this gap.

We wrote that sentence into the report about our own programme, because it is true. A programme of that design takes projects to the point of an investment decision and by construction cannot take them to a working system. Saying so is not a caveat. It is the reason the next engagement is a different engagement.

Four things were missing, and none of them was knowledge.

  • 01No approved environment. No corporate contour in which an employee can assemble an assistant through a standard procedure. Without one, every initiative becomes an IT ticket and the queue stops at around the twentieth.
  • 02No portfolio mechanism. No intake, no selection criteria, no funding route, and — most missing of all — no procedure for stopping a project. Five pilots can be run by hand. Fifty cannot.
  • 03No data competence. Not programmers: someone able to judge whether accounting-system data is fit for an assistant to work with, and prove it by measurement.
  • 04No measurement practice. The baseline problem above, generalised across the organisation.
What we recommended

Including our own interest in it.

Three options were set out: build the capability internally, install it with an external team, or appoint an internal portfolio owner immediately and buy external expertise for three bounded tasks. We recommended the third, and stated our own commercial interest in that recommendation in the report itself.

The reasoning: a pilot without an internal owner does not survive, whatever the quality of external support. The programme demonstrated it directly — the teams that advanced furthest were those where the process owner was named and attended every session.

Where this leads

Which is why we now start with measurement.

In a market where efficiency claims are routinely quoted without a source, publishing the limits of what we measured is the point rather than a weakness.
