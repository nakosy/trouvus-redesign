The 30-day baseline

Measure the process before you automate it.

Thirty days, one process: how long the work actually takes, measured rather than estimated. It is inexpensive, it takes a month, and it is the only honest foundation for any claim that follows — including ours.

Why this comes first

Not one team measured its own process.

In a ten-day executive programme delivered in August 2026, five cross-functional teams took five real processes to investment-decision quality.1 Every business case was arithmetically sound. Not one of the five had measured how long its process step actually takes. Durations were estimated from process description.

One team reported eight hours where the figure turned out to describe how long a document takes to travel, not how long a person works on it.

This is not a criticism of the participants. The organisation had no practice of operational measurement — durations were known by impression rather than by protocol. And the implication runs well past AI: where a baseline is not measured before work begins, the effect of any project becomes impossible to confirm or refute, and every argument about results collapses into an argument about opinions.

The diagnostic

Which work to offload, and which to protect.

Before measuring a process, it is worth knowing which side of the line it sits on. Drift the boundary and watch what happens — the failure is that nothing appears to happen, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed.

What the month looks like

One process, measured to protocol.

WEEK 01 — SCOPE

Choose one process and name the person answerable for it. Describe it as it actually runs rather than as the regulations say it runs — usually the first time that description has existed.

WEEK 02 — INSTRUMENT

Define what will be counted, by whom, and how. Agree the stop condition now, while nobody is invested in the answer.

WEEK 03 — MEASURE

Collect actual durations against the protocol. Not impressions, not recollection, not a figure someone remembers from a meeting.

WEEK 04 — REPORT

A measured baseline, the variance around it, and a plain statement of what the measurement does and does not support. Including, where it applies, that automation is not yet justified.

You may not like the answer, and that is the value. A baseline that shows a process is already efficient has saved you the cost of automating it. We would rather tell you that in month one than build something that cannot be defended in month nine.
What we found downstream

Training does not close this gap.

We wrote that sentence into the report to the client's owners, about our own programme, because it is true: a programme of that design takes projects to the point of an investment decision and by construction cannot take them to a working system.1

Four things were missing, and none of them was knowledge.

  • 01No approved environment. No corporate contour in which an employee can assemble an assistant through a standard procedure. Without one, every initiative becomes an IT ticket and the queue stops at around the twentieth.
  • 02No portfolio mechanism. No intake, no selection criteria, no funding route, and — most missing of all — no procedure for stopping a project. Five pilots can be run by hand. Fifty cannot.
  • 03No data competence. Not programmers: someone able to judge whether accounting-system data is fit for an assistant to work with, and prove it by measurement.
  • 04No measurement practice. The baseline problem above, generalised across the organisation.
